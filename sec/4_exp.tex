\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

An attribution read from attention is only a hypothesis about what the model used.
We test it by intervention and organize the experiments around four questions.
\textbf{RQ1}: do the units OmniTrace selects matter to the model, in every modality?
\textbf{RQ2}: how much of the faithfulness of perturbation methods does one pass recover, and at what cost?
\textbf{RQ3}: does the model cite what it uses?
\textbf{RQ4}: does one fixed setting transfer to other models?

\subsection{A Causal Test for Every Modality}
\label{sec:protocol}

\paragraph{Replay with hidden units.}
We keep the model's own response $\mathbf{y}$ fixed and score it with teacher forcing, $\ell(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_t\log P(y_t\mid\mathbf{x},y_{<t})$.
For a set of units $\mathcal{H}$, the \emph{drop} is $\Delta(\mathcal{H})=\ell(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})-\ell(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{\setminus\mathcal{H}})$ in nats per token, where $\mathbf{x}_{\setminus\mathcal{H}}$ is the input with the units in $\mathcal{H}$ hidden.

\paragraph{Hiding without a trace.}
Continuous inputs raise a problem that text does not: a second of audio cannot be deleted without shifting everything after it.
We replace a hidden unit \emph{at the input of the encoder} by a placeholder of the same length that carries no content: black frames, silence, a black image, or blank tokens.
Token counts, positions, and the time stamps of all other units stay the same.
With this operation, leave-one-out and ContextCite run on audio and video exactly as they do on text.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
Every number is relative to hiding the same count of random units, so that the amount hidden is controlled.
\emph{Lift} is the extra drop when a method's top-ranked units are hidden, $\Delta(\text{top})-\mathbb{E}\,\Delta(\text{random})$.
\emph{Keep gain} is the reverse test, in which everything except the selected units is hidden.
At sentence level we hide the top units of one sentence and measure the drop of that sentence. \emph{Sentence lift} compares it with random units, and \emph{sentence specificity} compares it with the mean drop of the other sentences, so it is positive only if the units matter for this sentence in particular.
Budgets are 10--50\% of the duration for audio and video and 1--3 of 15 quotes for documents; tables use 20\% and 2 quotes (1 quote at sentence level).
We report 95\% bootstrap intervals and compare methods by paired differences (\Cref{appendix:causal}).

\paragraph{Models and data.}
We use Qwen2.5-Omni-7B~\cite{xu2025qwen2}, MiniCPM-o~4.5~\cite{yu2025minicpm}, and Qwen3-Omni-30B~\cite{Xu2025Qwen3OmniTR} with greedy decoding (\Cref{tab:stats}). 
NExT-GQA~\cite{xiao2024nextgqa} provides video questions with human-marked evidence intervals; the model sees the frames only.
From MMAU~\cite{sakshi2025mmau} we take the audio questions whose answer depends on a part of the clip.
MMDocRAG~\cite{dong2025mmdocrag} gives each question 15 quotes from a long document, about ten text passages and five images, of which about 19\% are gold evidence.
The models reason step by step.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
All baselines rank the same units and pass through the same test.
Two cost about as little as OmniTrace: \emph{embedding similarity} between a sentence and each unit (SigLIP, CLAP, and a text encoder), and \emph{self-citation}, a second generation that asks the model to cite time spans or quote ids.
Two are perturbation methods: \emph{leave-one-out} hides each unit in turn, and \emph{ContextCite}~\cite{cohen-wang2024contextcite} fits a sparse linear model on 32 random ablations.
The perturbation baselines are fitted with the same hiding operation and the same target that the test measures, which favors them; we treat them as a practical upper bound.
One is trained: \emph{AT2}~\cite{cohenwang2025at2} reads the same attention as OmniTrace but learns one weight per head from the ContextCite ablations; we cross-fit it so that no question is scored by weights that saw its ablations.
Details are in \Cref{appendix:baselines}.

\subsection{RQ1: The Selected Units Matter}
\label{sec:rq1}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{media/figure/fig_curves.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Hiding what OmniTrace selects hurts the response more than hiding random units of the same count}, in every modality, at every budget, and on every model. Each line is the drop in the log-probability of a model's own response when a share of its input is hidden: orange when the hidden units are the ones OmniTrace ranks highest for that model, blue when they are drawn at random. Solid lines are Qwen2.5-Omni-7B, dashed lines MiniCPM-o~4.5, and dotted lines Qwen3-Omni-30B (its video run is not finished), each explaining its own responses with the same OmniTrace setting. Bands are 95\% bootstrap intervals.}
\label{fig:curves}
\end{figure*}

\Cref{fig:curves} shows the central result.
Across three modalities and three models, the curve for OmniTrace's units lies above the curve for random units at every budget, and no interval of the difference includes zero (\Cref{appendix:budgets}).
The reverse test agrees: keeping only the selected units preserves more of the response than keeping random ones, reliably so except at some video budgets.

The result also holds for single sentences (\Cref{tab:baselines}, S-lift and S-spec).
Hiding the units attributed to a sentence lowers that sentence's probability more than random units do, and more than it lowers the other sentences of the same response.
The attribution is therefore specific to the sentence, not a general map of what is salient in the input.
The labels of \Cref{eq:label}, which keep 10--22\% of the units, pass the deletion and sentence-level tests in all eight settings (\Cref{appendix:labels}).

Effects are large for documents, moderate for audio, and small for video, where neighboring seconds carry much of the same content.

\subsection{RQ2: Most of the Faithfulness, None of the Cost}
\label{sec:baselines}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{media/figure/fig_cost.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Faithfulness against cost} on Qwen2.5-Omni-7B. Lift of each method as a share of leave-one-out's lift, against the extra forward passes it needs per question. OmniTrace sits in the shaded column: it reads its scores from the generation pass. AT2 sits there too at test time, after training on perturbation data from the same model.}
\label{fig:cost}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Against methods of the same cost.}
OmniTrace has about twice the lift of embedding similarity in every modality (\Cref{tab:baselines}, \Cref{fig:cost}), and the gap widens at sentence level.
Units that look related to a sentence are not the units the model read: on audio the sentence specificity of embedding similarity is close to zero.

\paragraph{Against perturbation.}
Leave-one-out and ContextCite spend 16 to 41 extra forward passes per question to measure directly what our test measures.
From a single pass, OmniTrace reaches 96\% of leave-one-out's lift on video, 77\% on audio, and 95\% on image--text input, and the share grows with the budget (\Cref{appendix:budgets}).
At sentence level it keeps 83\% and 93\% of leave-one-out's lift on audio and image--text; video is the weak case, at about 60\%.
Perturbation remains the more faithful choice when its cost is acceptable. OmniTrace offers most of that faithfulness when it is not, the usual case for long audio and video.

\paragraph{Against a trained reader of attention.}
With equal weights on the heads of the late layers AT2 \emph{is} OmniTrace, so the comparison isolates what training on perturbation data adds.
On this model it adds little: OmniTrace is level with AT2 on video at every budget and reaches 95\% and 94\% of its lift on audio and image--text input, without the 32 ablation passes per training question and the new fit that AT2 needs for every model.

\paragraph{On a second model.}
We repeat every baseline on MiniCPM-o~4.5 (\Cref{appendix:minicpm}).
On audio the picture holds (72--95\% of leave-one-out's lift from a 20\% budget on, 1.4--2 times that of embedding similarity), and on video OmniTrace overtakes leave-one-out from 30\% on.
On image--text input it reaches only a quarter of the perturbation methods' lift, while AT2 stays close to them with four fifths of its weight outside the late layers: this model's generation pass holds the evidence too, but for documents not where the fixed setting reads it.

The cheapest evidence is the one the model states itself. We repeat the generation with a prompt that asks for a citation after each sentence and test the cited units (\Cref{fig:stated}).

\emph{Citations are often missing.} The model cites a valid source in 82\% of its document responses, but in only 38\% of audio and 31\% of video responses.

\emph{Cited units matter less.} Where the model does cite, hiding OmniTrace's units of the same count costs 3.1 times as much as hiding the cited units on audio and 1.7 times as much on documents; on video the two are level.
The citations also agree less with human evidence than OmniTrace's labels do (F1 of 0.22 against 0.48 on documents and 0.10 against 0.20 on video).

\emph{Citations follow the prompt.} Of the 120 audio responses that contain a time span, 32 repeat the example span of the instruction.
Asking for citations also changes the answer: video accuracy falls from 72.3\% to 55.3\%.
MiniCPM-o~4.5 cites more often, and its cited audio spans again matter less than OmniTrace's units (lift 0.064 against 0.098), while its cited video seconds and quotes matter more (\Cref{appendix:minicpm}): how far a citation can be trusted changes with the model and the modality.
Attribution read from the generation pass leaves the response unchanged and covers every sentence.

\subsection{RQ4: One Setting, Three Models}
\label{sec:variants}

The setting was fixed on Qwen2.5-Omni video data and applied without any change to MiniCPM-o~4.5, a model from another family with a different depth and a different tokenization of video, audio, and images, and to Qwen3-Omni-30B, a model four times as large.
All tests of RQ1 hold on both (\Cref{fig:curves}, last rows of \Cref{tab:baselines}).
On Qwen3-Omni the effects on documents are as large as on Qwen2.5-Omni, and the quotes OmniTrace selects matter more than the human-marked ones (lift 0.316 against 0.267).

Simplicity is what makes the transfer possible.
A hard-tracing variant with per-token argmax and vote filtering is level with OmniTrace on audio and documents for the model it was developed on, but on MiniCPM-o~4.5 it loses 86\% of its lift on audio and falls behind on documents (\Cref{appendix:variants}).
Its extra steps fit one model. The plain sum of late-layer attention mass has nothing to fit.
