A user also cares whether the evidence shown is evidence a person would accept, so we compare each ranking with the human-marked evidence (\Cref{tab:agreement}; \Cref{fig:example} shows one response).

On documents, OmniTrace agrees with the gold quotes better than every baseline, including the perturbation methods that are causally stronger. On correctly answered questions its top quote is gold in 90\% of the cases.
On video it has the best AUROC, but its single top second is evidence no more often than chance: attention finds the right region of the clip without pinning down the exact second.

The table also shows why agreement cannot be the main test: the perturbation methods have the largest causal effect and the lowest AUROC. What a model depends on and what a person marks overlap only in part.

\subsection{Unit Size and Modality Reliance}
\label{sec:size}

Mixed inputs make units unequal: an image quote takes hundreds of tokens and a passage a few dozen, and a larger unit can collect more mass.
On documents the scores of OmniTrace correlate with quote size at $\rho=0.51$, while the measured effects of leave-one-out and ContextCite correlate at 0.09 and 0.14 (\Cref{appendix:size}).
Dividing by the token count over-corrects ($\rho=-0.60$) and lowers the lift, so we keep the sum and report the bias.
OmniTrace-trained does not show the bias ($\rho=0.08$): a choice of heads, not a division by size, corrects it.

Across modalities the reading is accurate: images hold 74\% of the source tokens but receive 47\% of the attention mass, and leave-one-out, ContextCite, and OmniTrace-trained place 47--48\% of the measured effect on images.

The appendix adds two checks: merging one-second units into longer ones (\Cref{appendix:granularity}), and attention mass as a sign of how much a sentence depends on the sources (\Cref{appendix:flagging}).
