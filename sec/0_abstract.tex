\begin{abstract}
An omni-modal language model can answer a question about a document, a recording, or a video, but it cannot show which part of the input the answer came from.
Asking the model to cite its evidence yields what it \emph{says} it used; measuring what it \emph{used} has so far required rerunning it many times with parts of the input removed, a cost that grows with every second of audio and video.
We show that the generation pass already holds most of this information.
OmniTrace attributes each generated sentence to source units (text passages, images, and seconds of audio or video) by reading late-layer attention as mass over units, not as peaks over tokens, which is what makes attention usable for continuous inputs whose evidence is spread over hundreds of positions.
The method needs no training, no extra forward pass, and no per-modality tuning.
Because attention is only a hypothesis about what a model used, we pair the method with a causal test for continuous inputs: units are hidden behind content-free placeholders, the model's own response is replayed, and the effect is compared with hiding random units of the same count.
Across video, audio, and image--text question answering on three omni-modal models, the units OmniTrace selects are causally important for responses and for individual sentences.
On the model the method was developed on, one pass recovers 77--96\% of the effect of leave-one-out attribution, which needs up to 41 extra passes.
A model's own citations are a weaker guide: they can be missing from most audio and video responses, and cited audio spans matter less than the spans OmniTrace finds on both models where we test them.
\end{abstract}
